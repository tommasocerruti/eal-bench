\subsection{A restatement of a superseded permission is the trigger}
\label{sec:trigger}
The hand-written histories confound the features that could drive laundering, so we use the generated corpus (Section~\ref{subsec:benchmark-construction}), in which the three histories of a group differ only in how many later messages, from parties without authority, repeat a permission after it has been changed. Table~\ref{tab:trigger} pools the paper's three Baseten writers with GPT-OSS-120B as executor, 324 unauthorized requests per level. With no restatement, no writer forms a false permission on any request. With two, false authority forms for 15.4\% of unauthorized requests and 16.7\% are submitted; four restatements are not measurably different from two. Authorized use stays at or near 100\% throughout, so the failure on this corpus is laundering, not caution. Two further differences are observed on the same corpus but on histories that are not otherwise matched: histories that amend a grant in place form false authority about four times as often as histories that revoke and replace it (17.3\% against 4.5\%), and the failure rate shows no trend across gaps of one to three blocks between grant and change. The two added writers show the same shape (Appendix~\ref{app:generated-corpus}).
